Principles

Chess principles, explained.

Guidelines distilled by Throiy Chess. They are made for beginner to expert players. Every position is different, so when two principles clash, calculate the actual moves.

Start here

Control the centre in the opening

Beginner · Opening

Fight for the four central squares with pawns and pieces from the first moves. Central pieces cover the most squares and give you freedom to move.

Develop your minor pieces

Beginner · Opening

Bring your knights and bishops into the game early, each to a useful square. Do not move the same piece twice without a reason.

Castle early

Beginner · Opening

Do not play recklessly: get your king safe early in the opening by castling, before the middlegame breaks loose.

Trade only when it gains something

Beginner · All

Do not make pointless trades in the centre or the opening. If a trade gives you nothing tangible but helps the opponent develop their pieces (such as a queen or knight) with tempo, avoid it. Every trade needs a tactical or positional purpose.

Optimize: improve your worst piece

Beginner · All

When there is no check, capture or forcing threat, improve your passive pieces, starting with the ones still on your back rank (minor pieces first).

Checks, captures, threats: a fixed thinking system

Beginner · All

Run the same steps every move: Checks, Captures, Threats, then Optimize. It prevents blunders, stops you missing chances and gives consistently good moves. Use it for your opponent too: what checks, captures and threats can they play?

Look first, do not play too fast

Beginner · All

Before you move, stop at every move of your opponent. Ask: what is the purpose of this move, what would he do with a free move, and does he have checks or captures? Only then choose your own move.

Keep everything connected and nothing hanging

Beginner · All

Avoid isolated pawns and loose pieces far from the rest of your army. Let pawns and pieces protect each other and stay close, so tactical blunders are reduced to a minimum.

Ask after every move: what does he want?

Beginner · All

After each opponent move ask what the threat or plan is. Run the checks, captures and threats for your opponent as well as for yourself: what could he play next? Parry it in time, for example a pawn advancing in the centre or a threat on the wing, and close their break squares.

Activate your king in the endgame

Beginner · Endgame

In the endgame the king is a strong piece, not a target. Bring it to the centre to support your pawns and block the opponent.

Opening

Simple beats theoretical

Beginner · Opening

Choose openings built on sound principles rather than endless variations. You leave the opening with confidence and without memory slips.

A doubled pawn is not always weak

Intermediate · Opening

It becomes a problem when it is isolated and cannot be defended. If it takes squares from the opponent and controls the centre, it can be very useful.

Be flexible in the opening

Intermediate · Opening

You do not have to follow the main line to get a good position. A less common move order makes your opponent think for themselves from the start.

Control the centre in the opening

Beginner · Opening

Fight for the four central squares with pawns and pieces from the first moves. Central pieces cover the most squares and give you freedom to move.

Develop your minor pieces

Beginner · Opening

Bring your knights and bishops into the game early, each to a useful square. Do not move the same piece twice without a reason.

Be economical with your moves

Intermediate · Opening

Make every move the most active option and skip useless manoeuvres. Lost time in the opening hands your opponent a lead in development.

Castle early

Beginner · Opening

Do not play recklessly: get your king safe early in the opening by castling, before the middlegame breaks loose.

Flexible move order: centre and development first

Intermediate · Opening

Do not rush to finish a fixed pawn set-up, such as a pawn triangle. First control the centre and develop your minor pieces. The pawn structure can wait.

Pawn support frees your pieces

Intermediate · Opening

A solid pawn defence, such as c6 backing d5, takes defending jobs off your pieces. Once a centre pawn is covered by a pawn, the knight next to it is free to go on the attack.

Do not bring the queen out too early

Beginner · Opening

Do not rush the queen to the centre where enemy minor pieces can attack it. You lose tempo moving it again and again.

Watch for flank pawns challenging your centre

Intermediate · Opening

Be alert when your opponent tries to trade off your centre pawn with a flank pawn (such as ...c5) and claim the centre. Keep the option of recapturing with a centre pawn of your own.

Attack weak, misplaced pieces at once

Intermediate · Opening

Spot an opponent's misplaced piece, such as a knight on c6 played too early, and put maximum pressure on it with active pieces, for example a bishop on b5 and a queen on a4.

Win back the sacrificed pawn

Intermediate · Opening

If you sacrifice a pawn, play for the compensation (lead in development, initiative). Winning the pawn back is often a good option, but it is not a goal in itself.

Answer a4 with b4 to claim space (in some structures)

Expert · Opening

When your opponent tries to disturb you on the wing with a4, you can answer with b4 to claim space, take squares from the knights and strengthen the structure.

Avoid tricks and gambits as your main plan

Intermediate · Opening

Quick opening tricks (such as the Schiller Gambit or early queen attacks) look fun for a fast win, but in the long run they give bad positions, a lead in development for your opponent and a weak pawn structure.

Do not push pawns aimlessly

Beginner · Opening

Do not shove pawns around in the opening unless it helps control the centre or develop your minor pieces.

Finish development before you attack

Beginner · Opening

Do not attack too early (with premature bishop or pawn moves) when your position is not fully developed. Incomplete development leaves holes your opponent can punish tactically.

Trade only when it gains something

Beginner · All

Do not make pointless trades in the centre or the opening. If a trade gives you nothing tangible but helps the opponent develop their pieces (such as a queen or knight) with tempo, avoid it. Every trade needs a tactical or positional purpose.

Choose the active square over the default routine

Intermediate · Opening

Do not choose a square out of habit. Ask which square creates the most concrete pressure. A fianchetto is often right; sometimes Bd3 or Be2 is better.

Develop quickly: time matters

Beginner · Opening

Bring your pieces into the game quickly and directly. Do not waste precious time on distractions or aimless moves; focus on fast, efficient development, so you can outnumber the opponent later on.

Opening system: d4 and e3

Build a system opening

Intermediate · Opening

Use a fixed set-up that lets you play the same solid plan against almost any reply, so you have less theory to remember.

Use a flexible knight move to prepare e4

Intermediate · Opening

Nd2 instead of Nc3 keeps the c-pawn free and prepares e4, a break your opponent usually has to take into account.

Queen to b3 at the right moment

Intermediate · Opening

Qb3 can work when b7 is hard to defend and the queen cannot be driven away with tempo. Check that first: ...Na5, ...Nb4 or ...Bf5 can hit the queen.

Hunt the defending bishop

Intermediate · Opening

If your opponent puts a bishop on f5 or g4 to stop your e4 break, force a trade or chase it with a knight move to h4 to weaken their defence.

Bring the queen to the centre

Intermediate · Opening

Once the enemy bishop is off the diagonal, you can move the queen (often after a trade) to the centre to control e4 and raise the pressure.

Punish passive play with a direct break

Intermediate · Middlegame

Against passive play, prepare and then open the centre with e4 when your pieces are ready.

Create deadly forks in the middlegame

Intermediate · Middlegame

Keep a sharp eye for tactical moments where a centralized knight or an active queen attacks a king position and pieces at the same time.

Keep looking at 'poisoned' pawns

Intermediate · Middlegame

If your opponent leaves a pawn undefended or open to capture, check whether it is poisoned by a pin or a mate threat before you take it. Punish them with the material loss if it is.

Choose a reliable, repeatable system: the Italian Game

Intermediate · Opening

Play an opening such as the Italian Game (1.e4 e5 2.Nf3 Nc6 3.Bc4), which suits every level from beginner to grandmaster. You play on flexible plans and development patterns instead of memorizing endless variations.

A knight plan in the Italian Game

Intermediate · Opening

Use the knight manoeuvre Nbd2-f1-g3 in slow Italian set-ups. Bring the c1 bishop out when it has a useful square, often e3 or g5.

A simple, universal Black system against 1.d4

Intermediate · Opening

As Black, pick a reliable set-up (such as the Queen's Gambit Declined with ...Nf6 and ...e6/...d5) that you can use against almost any White move, so you do not have to learn thousands of variations.

Develop the kingside first, then castle

Intermediate · Opening

In this set-up develop your kingside minor pieces (knight and bishop) first and get your king safe with short castling in time.

Challenge the centre from the flank (...c5)

Expert · Opening

Support your position with flexible pawn structures (such as ...b6, ...Bb7 and the break ...c5) to raise the pressure on White's centre and open lines.

Sicilian Defence

Choose the Sicilian Defence for a dynamic position

Intermediate · Opening

With 1...c5 Black fights for the centre indirectly, stops White from setting up two central pawns side by side, and creates an asymmetric, aggressive game.

Play the flexible ...e6 against side lines

Intermediate · Opening

Against early side lines such as Bc4, ...e6 is a very solid, low-theory set-up for Black.

Hit the centre with ...d5 when White is inaccurate

Intermediate · Opening

When White plays suboptimal bishop or pawn moves, answer at once with ...d5 to open the centre and chase the enemy bishop.

Develop by the basic principles, even in a sharp opening

Intermediate · Opening

Even in a sharp opening like the Sicilian the fundamentals apply: develop your minor pieces, control the centre and get your king safe in time (often with short castling).

Use the half-open c-file and the queen on c7

Expert · Opening

The queen on c7 is thematic in the Open Sicilian: it eyes the c-file and supports ...b5 and ...a6 expansion. Watch for Nb5 and Nd5 tricks.

Neutralize early queen attacks with simplicity

Intermediate · Opening

Early queen tricks against the Sicilian (such as Qh5 or Qf3) are easily parried with ...e6. The exposed queen can then lose time to your developing knights.

Be careful with an early ...e5 in the Sicilian

Expert · Opening

In the early opening ...e5 leaves a hole on d5 that White's pieces control. Check before you play it.

Answering the early bishop pin

Answer an early bishop pin with ...h6

Intermediate · Opening

When a bishop pins your knight on f6 (or f3) early with Bg5 (or Bg4), ask the question at once with ...h6. If the bishop stays on the pin, you can break it with ...g5.

Lock the bishop in and switch to the other wing

Expert · Opening

If after ...g5 the bishop is driven back to a passive corner such as g3, where your pawns and its own pawns block it, it is out of play. Block that side and move the initiative to the other wing, where you have more pieces.

Strengthen the centre before the flank can exploit the pin

Intermediate · Opening

Before the opponent profits on the wing from the pin, strengthen the centre with flexible moves such as ...c5 or ...e5, so they have no counterplay there. Centre control neutralizes a flank attack on a pinned piece.

Reposition a knight to chase the bishop (Nf1-e3)

Intermediate · Opening

If the opponent keeps the bishop and you want to increase the pressure, use a classic knight route (such as via f1 to e3) to chase it away actively and regroup harmoniously, without weakening your pawn structure.

Middlegame plan

Pick a middlegame plan

Intermediate · Middlegame

After the opening, choose one clear aim, such as a specific pawn break. A plan gives direction to every following move and puts the opponent under pressure.

Prepare the break, then play it

Intermediate · Middlegame

Play a thematic pawn break once your pieces stand well to support it. Breaking too early loses pawns; patient preparation slowly squeezes the opponent.

Block the line instead of adding a defender

Intermediate · Middlegame

A pawn under attack does not always need another defender. Sometimes you can cut the attacking line. That keeps your pieces active instead of passive.

Simple, controlled play wins

Intermediate · Middlegame

Simple, controlled play wins many games at club level. Avoid unsound sacrifices and convert small advantages.

Optimize: improve your worst piece

Beginner · All

When there is no check, capture or forcing threat, improve your passive pieces, starting with the ones still on your back rank (minor pieces first).

Improve with a purpose

Intermediate · All

When you reposition a piece such as your queen, prefer an active square that attacks a target (an undefended piece or a pawn) over a passive retreat.

Attack the backward pawn

Intermediate · Middlegame

A pawn that cannot be supported by other pawns and cannot advance is a weakness. Pile your pressure on it and try to win it step by step.

Build up pressure, then break

Intermediate · Middlegame

When your opponent's pieces are all tied to defending one weakness, create the breaking move (such as an advancing pawn) to open the position. The defence often cracks.

Pawns leave holes behind

Expert · Middlegame

A pawn that moves forward can never protect the square it left. When pawns advance too far, holes appear where the opponent can plant a piece for good.

Prophylaxis: control the key squares

Expert · All

Cut off the opponent's counterplay by controlling the key invasion squares before they get there. A key square held in advance leaves them with nothing to do.

After you win material, check their compensation

Intermediate · Middlegame

Ask what the opponent got in return, for example the bishop pair. Then trade off that specific advantage.

Optimize with a pawn move (gain space)

Expert · Middlegame

A pawn move can also be an improving move, if it seriously restricts the opponent's pieces or takes extra space, for example ...c6 against the opponent's pieces.

Activate a passive piece in time

Intermediate · All

If one of your pieces, such as a rook, has stood passively on the back rank through the whole middlegame, look for a moment to activate it for a break or to pressure the enemy weaknesses.

Create a weak pawn in the enemy camp

Intermediate · Middlegame

Trade pieces or open lines in a way that leaves your opponent with a weak, backward or isolated pawn, then keep permanent pressure on it.

Read the pawn structure as a skeleton

Intermediate · Middlegame

Read the structure to see which squares and routes are ideal for your pieces, such as a knight route to f4.

Park the queen behind your pieces (a8 or b7)

Expert · Middlegame

In some closed structures the queen can go to secondary squares such as a8 behind your own pieces, where it builds a battery on a diagonal or file without being exposed to a trade.

Keep control of the break squares (e5, c5)

Expert · Middlegame

In open or semi-open positions, use your active pieces to stop your opponent from making space-gaining breaks in the centre, such as ...e5 or ...c5.

Check that your pieces do not get in each other's way

Intermediate · Middlegame

The higher you climb, the less standard development is enough. Calculate that your pieces do not block each other and that you do not block flexible plans too early (such as a flank break with c4 or d4).

Target weak pawns before risky king attacks

Intermediate · Middlegame

Instead of a reckless king attack, put steady pressure on the opponent's weak pawns with your pieces and rooks. This builds your advantage gradually without needless risk.

Use the centre to restrict your opponent

Intermediate · Middlegame

A strong presence in the centre, for example a knight on an outpost, takes away the room your opponent's bishops, knights and rooks need to move. Centre control is not only for your own development, but also to limit theirs.

Avoid trades when you have a space advantage

Intermediate · Middlegame

When you have more space or hold the opponent in a bind, avoid needless trades: trades give the cramped side air and open lines for counterplay. Trade only if you get something out of it.

Use space to switch your heavy pieces quickly

Intermediate · Middlegame

With more space you can move your rooks and queen easily from one wing to the other, while the opponent is stuck for room. Use that space as a transport route to concentrate on their weak spots.

Drive knights back with pawns

Intermediate · Middlegame

Pawn advances that deny a knight its outposts can restrict it. Check that the advance does not weaken your own squares.

Put rooks on open files

Beginner · Middlegame

A rook is a linear piece that gains enormous power on an open or half-open file. Look for ways to manoeuvre your rooks to open files and dominate the edges and depth of the board.

Know the three kinds of weaknesses

Intermediate · Middlegame

There are three main weaknesses: weak colour complexes (diagonals not covered by your own bishops), weak squares or holes (often outposts for knights), and weak pawns (doubled, isolated and backward pawns). Fight each with the right piece.

Space is decided by your pawn chain

Expert · Middlegame

Space advantage is set by your pawn chain, the "mountain tops". The side with the most space controls the area behind its pawns and dictates where the action takes place. Play and attack on the part of the board where your pawn chain points and where you have the most space.

Tactics and king safety

Checks, captures, threats: a fixed thinking system

Beginner · All

Run the same steps every move: Checks, Captures, Threats, then Optimize. It prevents blunders, stops you missing chances and gives consistently good moves. Use it for your opponent too: what checks, captures and threats can they play?

Beware a free piece when a rook pawn opens a file at your king

Beginner · Middlegame

Do not just grab a 'free' piece when the opponent pushes a rook pawn to open a file towards your king. An offer like this opens attack lines, and your position can collapse in a few moves.

Deal with infiltrators on your half

Intermediate · Middlegame

Do not leave enemy pieces on your side of the board alone. Trade or chase them early, otherwise they become the base for dangerous combinations against your king.

Back-rank safety

Beginner · All

Watch for mate threats on the back rank, for you and for your opponent. Make a flight square (luft) for your king in time.

Master both kinds of blunders

Beginner · All

Avoid giving away your own material (or walking into mate), and avoid missing the chances your opponent offers you. Both kinds of carelessness cost you the game.

Look first, do not play too fast

Beginner · All

Before you move, stop at every move of your opponent. Ask: what is the purpose of this move, what would he do with a free move, and does he have checks or captures? Only then choose your own move.

Solve immediate problems first

Beginner · All

When your opponent creates a threat, such as an attack on a piece or a mate threat, solving it comes before your own attack plan.

Set a trap with a defensive move

Intermediate · Middlegame

Sometimes a defensive or passive-looking move (such as moving a rook) is a clever trap. The opponent is punished when he tries to grab your poisoned pawn.

Tactics beat opening theory

Intermediate · All

Most games below master level are decided by tactical blunders, not by opening theory. Spend your time reducing mistakes rather than memorizing tricks.

Look for in-between moves against a pin or threat

Intermediate · Middlegame

When your opponent sets up an awkward pin or threat (such as a queen move pressing on a diagonal), look for strong in-between moves or counterattacks, such as a mate threat or an attack on a piece, to force them onto the defensive.

Keep everything connected and nothing hanging

Beginner · All

Avoid isolated pawns and loose pieces far from the rest of your army. Let pawns and pieces protect each other and stay close, so tactical blunders are reduced to a minimum.

Ask after every move: what does he want?

Beginner · All

After each opponent move ask what the threat or plan is. Run the checks, captures and threats for your opponent as well as for yourself: what could he play next? Parry it in time, for example a pawn advancing in the centre or a threat on the wing, and close their break squares.

Tactics flow from a superior position

Intermediate · Middlegame

Do not hunt desperately for wild combinations or loose tricks. First build a strong, dominant position with space, harmony and active pieces; then the winning tactical blows tend to appear by themselves.

Do not weaken your king shelter lightly

Beginner · All

Castling and keeping the pawns in front of your king in place are about king safety. Do not weaken the king's position for a small material gain, unless enough of your own pieces are close to defend a weakened king.

Active Chess

Play actively and take the initiative

Intermediate · Middlegame

Take the initiative, put your pieces on their best squares, dominate the centre and claim space, without becoming reckless.

Play forcing, active moves in the opening

Intermediate · Opening

Choose opening moves that develop a minor piece and force an active answer, instead of slow, waiting moves.

Do not defend passively out of fear

Intermediate · Middlegame

Do not play waiting moves out of vague fear, but do respond to concrete threats and prevent the opponent's best plan.

Castle long only when it is safe

Intermediate · Opening

Long castling is the most aggressive form, but only castle long when the position allows it and your king is really safe there.

Keep fighting for the centre

Beginner · Middlegame

Keep the centre in mind and fight for it while it is relevant, by aiming pieces at it or advancing centre pawns. Switch to concrete threats when needed.

Defend aggressively

Intermediate · Middlegame

When you have to defend, for example against an attack on your queen, try to answer with an attack of your own on an enemy pawn or piece.

Calculate sacrifices three moves deep

Expert · Middlegame

Calculate a sacrifice as deeply and concretely as you can. If you cannot see clear compensation, do not play it.

Open the king with a pawn storm

Expert · Middlegame

Advance your pawns (such as the g and h pawns) against the enemy king to open lines and files for your rooks.

Fix problems with pieces, not pawns

Intermediate · Middlegame

Prefer piece moves when fixing problems, since pawn moves cannot be undone. A pawn move is fine when it solves a concrete issue (such as h3 or a luft).

Do not panic and weaken

Beginner · Middlegame

When attacked, do not make weakening pawn moves out of panic. Defend accurately and counterattack only if it is really faster.

Force reactions with early threats

Intermediate · Middlegame

Make threats that improve your position while forcing a reaction. Avoid threats that are simply parried, because they only waste time.

Avoid hope chess

Beginner · All

Do not play a move hoping your opponent blunders (such as leaving a bishop hanging). Play on objective pressure and concrete calculation.

Keep track of the material balance

Intermediate · Middlegame

Keep the material balance clearly in your head. Every pawn won or lost steers your strategic choices. Being greedy is only fine when it is safe.

Use advanced pawns as wedges

Expert · Middlegame

Advanced pawns can cramp the opponent when they are well supported and your king is not at risk. Calculate before you push.

Activity can be worth more than material

Expert · Middlegame

Activity can compensate for material when it creates concrete threats. Check it with calculation, and check that the opponent cannot safely return the material.

Judge activity by squares controlled and mobility

Intermediate · Middlegame

Activity means the number of squares you control and the dynamic value of your pieces. If you win a passive rook or piece in a corner but lose your active bishops and knights for it, you give away valuable dominance. Keep active pieces on the board and push the opponent into a passive role.

Attack and defence

Opposite-side castling is a race

Intermediate · Middlegame

When the kings castle on opposite sides, speed and mating attacks matter most. The player who starts a pawn storm first, with enough pieces behind it, usually gets the better of it.

Support the pawn storm

Intermediate · Middlegame

A pawn storm works best when the leading pawn is supported by pieces or pawns, so that lines really open.

Answer a wing attack in the centre

Intermediate · Middlegame

If a faster pawn storm hits one wing, look for counterplay by opening the centre. This often changes the course of the game and defuses the wing attack.

Open lines by trading pawns

Intermediate · Middlegame

A pawn storm aims to open lines for your rooks and queen by trading pawns. Removing the pawns around the enemy king exposes the defence and makes quick mating patterns possible.

Prepare the pawn storm

Expert · Middlegame

Do not push a pawn when the opponent can place a blocking knight in front of it and slow you down. Check that knight's landing square before you move the pawn mass forward.

Exchange sacrifice on the wing

Expert · Middlegame

An exchange sacrifice (rook for a minor piece) can break open the king position when it gives lasting attacking chances.

Tighten the net with flank pawns (f3, g4, h4)

Expert · Middlegame

Flank pawn advances can trap a piece or gain space when your own king is safe. Check concrete lines first.

Coordinate knights and rooks for mate

Intermediate · Middlegame

Bring knights and rooks along open files to advanced squares (such as f4) to create deadly mating patterns on the back rank or around the enemy king.

Be patient in an attack

Intermediate · Middlegame

Sometimes you should prepare a position calmly (for example with g6 or a quiet regrouping) to build maximum pressure, rather than playing hasty forcing tactics the opponent can parry.

Watch for long-range snipers

Intermediate · Middlegame

Always watch for distant pieces (a bishop or rook on a long diagonal or file) that strike from afar, to avoid tactical blunders and back-rank problems.

Clear the way for a kingside attack (f4 or d4)

Expert · Middlegame

Prepare the f4 break (or a central d4 break) in closed positions to activate your pieces.

Use the bishop pair against weak dark squares

Expert · Middlegame

If you keep the bishop pair, or trade off the enemy defenders, you can invade the weakened squares (such as the dark squares around the enemy king) step by step.

Use opposite-coloured bishops as an attacking weapon

Expert · Middlegame

In a middlegame with opposite-coloured bishops, your bishop works like an extra attacker that your opponent cannot challenge. Use it to find and hit weaknesses around the enemy king.

Bring your rooks into the game

Beginner · Middlegame

In the heat of an attack players often forget their rooks on a1 or h1. Make sure every piece joins the attack, and before a serious attack check that your rooks and heavy pieces have left their starting squares.

Do not respect an unsupported attack

Intermediate · Middlegame

If the opponent sets up a threat or pin early with a single piece (such as an isolated bishop) while the rest of the army stays passive, do not panic or turn passive. An attack is only dangerous when they outnumber you there. Count the real attackers and defenders on that part of the board first, then respond calmly.

Trade off the key attackers

Intermediate · Middlegame

When the opponent builds pressure with only a few active pieces, the quickest road to safety is to trade exactly those pieces. Once the attackers are gone, the attack collapses and you can take over the initiative.

Plant a piece deep in the enemy camp

Expert · Middlegame

A well-supported piece on an outpost deep in the enemy camp can paralyse the opponent. Check first that it cannot be removed.

In an attack, time can be worth more than material

Expert · Middlegame

The aim of a sacrifice is often not direct mate but time: slowing the opponent's development and locking their position in passivity. Accept a material sacrifice when it forces the opponent to spend tempi on defence instead of development.

Count the pieces in the fight, not on the board

Expert · Middlegame

Do not worry about lost pawns or pieces if the pieces that remain are passive spectators. If you concentrate all your active forces on one wing, you win the numbers there. Judge a position by how many active pieces take part, not only by the material balance.

Launch a massive attack only when you outnumber the defenders

Intermediate · Middlegame

Start a large attack not because you feel like it, but when you can show that you have more attackers than they have defenders near the king. Build up methodically, then open the position.

Punish a king stuck in the centre

Intermediate · Middlegame

If the opponent fails to castle in time and leaves the king in the middle, often behind a weakened pawn shield such as e6 and f7, act at once: open the centre and create strong threats, with a sacrifice if needed.

Eliminate the key defenders of the king

Intermediate · Middlegame

An attack often fails because specific key pieces (such as a bishop or a defending knight) hold the enemy king together. To make the attack succeed, deliberately remove or deflect those key defenders.

Use a pawn as an umbrella when under pressure

Expert · Middlegame

Under pressure, a well-defended pawn can block enemy lines or cramp attackers. Check that it does not become a target.

Converting and endgames

Trade pieces when ahead

Beginner · All

With a material lead, trading pieces is the fastest way to win. Fewer pieces make it easier to cash in. Trade pieces, not pawns.

Create a passed pawn

Beginner · Endgame

Use a pawn majority on one wing to create a passed pawn. A passed pawn ties down the opponent and often decides the endgame.

Rook versus minor piece: win it with technique

Expert · Endgame

Being up the exchange is a real edge, worth about 1.5 to 2 pawns. Build calmly, activate your king and use the rook to pick up pawns.

Activate your king in the endgame

Beginner · Endgame

In the endgame the king is a strong piece, not a target. Bring it to the centre to support your pawns and block the opponent.

Push your passed pawn

Beginner · Endgame

Push the passed pawn when it is supported and cannot be stopped easily. Passed pawns must be pushed, but with piece support.

Put your pawns on the opposite colour of the enemy bishop

Expert · Endgame

Against a bishop, pawns on the opposite colour are safe from it. Keep your own pawns on the colour opposite your own bishop.

Mate with king and queen

Beginner · Endgame

Shrink the box around the enemy king with your queen a knight's move away, bring your own king up, and finish with the 'kiss of death' or a corner mate. Check for stalemate on every move.

Trade queens when you are a pawn up

Beginner · Endgame

With a pawn extra, trading queens is often a good way to simplify and make a setback unlikely.

Pieces first, then pawns

Beginner · Endgame

Usually activate your king and pieces first. When a pawn race decides, count it concretely.

Fewer pawn islands

Intermediate · Endgame

Fewer pawn islands are easier to defend. Keep your pawns connected.

Beware opposite-coloured bishops

Intermediate · Endgame

Be careful trading into an endgame with opposite-coloured bishops when you are only one pawn up. The defender can often draw easily.

Castling is not automatic without queens

Expert · Endgame

When queens are off, your king is in less danger. Do not castle out of habit: use the king in the centre to fight and support pawns.

Doubled isolated pawns are a target

Intermediate · Endgame

Aim for positions where the opponent gets doubled, isolated pawns. They are hard to defend and easy to attack in the endgame.

Shelter your king from checks

Intermediate · Endgame

In rook endings, put your king behind your own pawns so the enemy rook cannot keep giving checks.

Watch out for stalemate

Beginner · Endgame

When your pawn is about to promote and the enemy king is boxed in, make sure your opponent still has a legal move.

Stay careful until the last move

Beginner · Endgame

Even in a won endgame one slip can throw away the win or allow a draw. Keep calculating and look for the cleanest forced finish.

March a pawn majority

Intermediate · Endgame

With a pawn majority on the queenside (4 against 2, for example), use it to advance your pawns step by step into a passed pawn and force your opponent into passive defence.

Keep the opponent's pieces passive

Expert · Endgame

When the opponent's rook is tied to passive defence, trade off their active pieces and centralise your own. Steer towards an endgame where the passive piece stays a problem for them.

Trade down when the structure is equal

Intermediate · Endgame

Trade down when the endgame favours you (better king, structure or activity), or when a draw is acceptable.

Convert with an outside passed pawn

Intermediate · Endgame

With extra pawns in a late middlegame, trade in a controlled way, create a distant (outside) passed pawn and force your opponent onto the defensive.

Attack the opponent's pawns in the endgame

Intermediate · Endgame

In the endgame there is usually too little attacking material left to mate. Pawns cannot run away, so make the weak or isolated pawns of the opponent the target of your rooks and king.

Prefer the bishop with pawns on both wings

Intermediate · Endgame

In endgames with pawns on both sides of the board a bishop is usually better than a knight, because it works on both wings at once and across the whole board, while a knight can operate on only one side at a time.

Practical psychology

Win or learn

All · All

Do not let a missed chance or a lost game spoil your enjoyment. Analyze the missed tactics calmly afterwards (such as hidden rook traps or missed in-between moves) to come back stronger.

Set a fixed number of games per session

All · All

Decide before you start how many games you will play, and stick to it. This stops you from playing on out of frustration or revenge after a loss (tilt) and keeps you fresh and focused.

Set a loss budget

All · All

Accept that you will lose roughly as often as you win against opponents of your own strength. Losses are your best teachers. Expecting them helps you recover faster and brood less.

Understanding is not the same as executing

All · All

Understanding a master move or plan in a video does not mean you can play it yourself over the board. Mistakes happen at every level, even among grandmasters, so keep training.

Choose a growth mindset

All · All

Do not identify with your results. Enjoy the process of improving your visualisation, calculation and pattern recognition more than the score.

Analyse your lost and difficult games

All · All

Real curiosity shows after the game. If you skip analysis and only play for points, you miss the chance to improve in a structural way and to see where the position went wrong.

Stay calm against an unfamiliar early gambit

All · All

Do not panic against a strange or rare gambit. Often you can take the offered pawn, solidify (for example with c3) and send your pieces back to safe squares.

Make the opponent's life uncomfortable

All · All

You do not always need the very best computer move. Among good moves, prefer the one that is practically and psychologically hardest for the opponent. Ask questions with active, provocative moves that test the defence.

Create confusion with a complicating sacrifice

All · All

Complicating sacrifices are a practical weapon only when you have checked that they are at worst objectively playable.
