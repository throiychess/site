Principles

Chess principles, explained.

Guidelines from coach Peter, distilled by Throiy Chess. They are made for beginner to expert players. Every position is different, so when two principles clash, calculate the actual moves.

Opening

Simple beats theoretical

Choose openings built on sound principles rather than endless variations. You leave the opening with confidence and without memory slips.

A doubled pawn is not always weak

It becomes a problem when it is isolated and cannot be defended. If it takes squares from the opponent and controls the centre, it can be very useful.

Be flexible in the opening

You do not have to follow the main line to get a good position. A less common move order makes your opponent think for themselves from the start.

The three opening principles

Control the centre, develop your minor pieces and get your king safe. That is what the opening is for. It saves you from aimless moves.

Be economical with your moves

Make every move the most active option and skip useless manoeuvres. Lost time in the opening hands your opponent a lead in development.

Castle early

Do not play recklessly: get your king safe early in the opening by castling, before the middlegame breaks loose.

Flexible move order: centre and development first

Do not rush to finish a fixed pawn set-up, such as a pawn triangle. First control the centre and develop your minor pieces. The pawn structure can wait.

Pawn support frees your pieces

A solid pawn defence, such as c6 backing d5, takes defending jobs off your pieces. Once a centre pawn is covered by a pawn, the knight next to it is free to go on the attack.

Do not bring the queen out too early

Do not rush the queen to the centre where enemy minor pieces can attack it. You lose tempo moving it again and again.

Watch for flank pawns challenging your centre

Be alert when your opponent tries to trade off your centre pawn with a flank pawn (such as ...c5) and claim the centre. Keep the option of recapturing with a centre pawn of your own.

Attack weak, misplaced pieces at once

Spot an opponent's misplaced piece, such as a knight on c6 played too early, and put maximum pressure on it with active pieces, for example a bishop on b5 and a queen on a4.

Opening system: d4 and e3

Build a system opening

Use a fixed set-up that lets you play the same solid plan against almost any reply, so you have less theory to remember.

Use a flexible knight move to prepare e4

Playing the knight to d2 instead of c3 prepares the strong central break e4, which forces your opponent to answer with a specific move such as ...d5.

Queen to b3 at the right moment

In this type of opening, an early queen move to b3 puts pressure on the undefended b7 pawn, adds force in the centre and helps your bishop development.

Hunt the defending bishop

If your opponent puts a bishop on f5 or g4 to stop your e4 break, force a trade or chase it with a knight move to h4 to weaken their defence.

Bring the queen to the centre

Once the enemy bishop is off the diagonal, you can move the queen (often after a trade) to the centre to control e4 and raise the pressure.

Punish passive play with a direct break

If your opponent plays passively, punish it at once by opening the centre with e4, or by letting a knight jump in for a fork.

Create deadly forks in the middlegame

Keep a sharp eye for tactical moments where a centralized knight or an active queen attacks a king position and pieces at the same time.

Keep looking at 'poisoned' pawns

If your opponent leaves a pawn undefended or open to capture, check whether it is poisoned by a pin or a mate threat before you take it. Punish them with the material loss if it is.

Middlegame plan

Pick a middlegame plan

After the opening, choose one clear aim, such as a specific pawn break. A plan gives direction to every following move and puts the opponent under pressure.

Prepare the break, then play it

Play a thematic pawn break once your pieces stand well to support it. Breaking too early loses pawns; patient preparation slowly squeezes the opponent.

Block the line instead of adding a defender

A pawn under attack does not always need another defender. Sometimes you can cut the attacking line. That keeps your pieces active instead of passive.

Simple, controlled play wins

Choose a simple, controlled style. Winning a single pawn is often enough. Sacrificing pieces without calculating usually just loses material.

Optimize: improve your worst piece

When there is no check, capture or forcing threat, improve your passive pieces, starting with the ones still on your back rank (minor pieces first).

Improve with a purpose

When you reposition a piece such as your queen, prefer an active square that attacks a target (an undefended piece or a pawn) over a passive retreat.

Attack the backward pawn

A pawn that cannot be supported by other pawns and cannot advance is a weakness. Pile your pressure on it and try to win it step by step.

Build up pressure, then break

When your opponent's pieces are all tied to defending one weakness, create the breaking move (such as an advancing pawn) to open the position. The defence often cracks.

Pawns leave holes behind

A pawn that moves forward can never protect the square it left. When pawns advance too far, holes appear where the opponent can plant a piece for good.

Prophylaxis: control the key squares

Cut off the opponent's counterplay by controlling the key invasion squares before they get there. A key square held in advance leaves them with nothing to do.

After you win material, check their compensation

Ask what the opponent got in return, for example the bishop pair. Then trade off that specific advantage.

Optimize with a pawn move (gain space)

A pawn move can also be an improving move, if it seriously restricts the opponent's pieces or takes extra space, for example ...c6 against the opponent's pieces.

Activate a passive piece in time

If one of your pieces, such as a rook, has stood passively on the back rank through the whole middlegame, look for a moment to activate it for a break or to pressure the enemy weaknesses.

Create a weak pawn in the enemy camp

Trade pieces or open lines in a way that leaves your opponent with a weak, backward or isolated pawn, then keep permanent pressure on it.

Tactics and king safety

A fixed thinking system: CCTO

Run the same steps every move: Checks, Captures, Threats, then Optimize. It prevents blunders, stops you missing chances and gives consistently good moves. Use it for your opponent too: what checks, captures and threats can they play?

Checks, captures, threats (CCT)

Look for the best move in a structured way: checks, captures and threats, for you and for your opponent. Doing this every move finds tactics that are really there and avoids blunders.

Spot the Fishing Pole Trap pattern

Do not just grab a 'free' piece when the opponent pushes a rook pawn to open a file towards your king. An offer like this opens attack lines, and your position can collapse in a few moves.

Deal with infiltrators on your half

Do not leave enemy pieces on your side of the board alone. Trade or chase them early, otherwise they become the base for dangerous combinations against your king.

Back-rank safety

Watch for mate threats on the back rank, for you and for your opponent. Make a flight square (luft) for your king in time.

Master both kinds of blunders

Avoid giving away your own material (or walking into mate), and avoid missing the chances your opponent offers you. Both kinds of carelessness cost you the game.

Sit on your hands

Stop at every move of your opponent. Ask: what is the purpose of this move, what would he do with a free move, and does he have checks or captures?

Solve immediate problems first

When your opponent creates a threat, such as an attack on a piece or a mate threat, solving it comes before your own attack plan.

Set a trap with a defensive move

Sometimes a defensive or passive-looking move (such as moving a rook) is a clever trap. The opponent is punished when he tries to grab your poisoned pawn.

Active Chess

Play actively and take the initiative

Take the initiative, put your pieces on their best squares, dominate the centre and claim space, without becoming reckless.

Play forcing, active moves in the opening

Choose opening moves that develop a minor piece and force an active answer, instead of slow, waiting moves.

Do not defend passively out of fear

Do not answer what your opponent might do with waiting moves, such as needless edge pawn moves. Carry on with your own plan.

Build a lead in development

Bring your minor pieces and queen to their best squares quickly, so you soon lead in development against a slow opponent.

Castle long only when it is safe

Long castling is the most aggressive form, but only castle long when the position allows it and your king is really safe there.

Keep fighting for the centre

Let every move contribute to control of the four central squares, by aiming pieces at them or advancing centre pawns.

Do not react to non-forcing moves

If your opponent makes a move without a direct threat, do not react straight away. Carry on with your own active plan.

Defend aggressively

When you have to defend, for example against an attack on your queen, try to answer with an attack of your own on an enemy pawn or piece.

Calculate sacrifices three moves deep

Before you sacrifice a piece, force yourself to calculate at least three moves. If you have not gained anything by then, the sacrifice is probably wrong or too complex.

Open the king with a pawn storm

Advance your pawns (such as the g and h pawns) against the enemy king to open lines and files for your rooks.

Fix problems with pieces, not pawns

When your king or position is under pressure, solve it by moving a piece rather than pushing a pawn. Pawn moves create permanent weaknesses ('hook pawns').

Do not panic and weaken

When your opponent attacks, do not weaken your own pawn structure. Keep your attacking tempo and keep pushing forward.

Force reactions with early threats

Play so your opponent has to answer your threats all the time. Then he has no time for his own plans.

Avoid hope chess

Do not play a move hoping your opponent blunders (such as leaving a bishop hanging). Play on objective pressure and concrete calculation.

Attack and defence

Opposite-side castling is a race

When the kings castle on opposite sides, speed and mating attacks matter most. The player who starts a pawn storm first, with enough pieces behind it, usually gets the better of it.

Support the pawn storm

A pawn storm on the wing works best when the leading pawn is supported by another pawn or piece. Support costs less time than waiting for the opponent to regroup.

Answer a wing attack in the centre

If a faster pawn storm hits one wing, look for counterplay by opening the centre. This often changes the course of the game and defuses the wing attack.

Open lines by trading pawns

A pawn storm aims to open lines for your rooks and queen by trading pawns. Removing the pawns around the enemy king exposes the defence and makes quick mating patterns possible.

Prepare the pawn storm

Do not push a pawn when the opponent can place a blocking knight in front of it and slow you down. Check that knight's landing square before you move the pawn mass forward.

Exchange sacrifice on the wing

A rook sacrifice against a defending wing pawn can break open the enemy king position and give your other pieces ways in.

Tighten the net with flank pawns (f3, g4, h4)

Use pawns such as f3, g4 or h4 to tighten the net around enemy pieces, such as a trapped knight or bishop, and to control squares.

Converting and endgames

Trade pieces when ahead

With a material lead, trading pieces is the fastest way to win. Fewer pieces make it easier to cash in. Trade pieces, not pawns.

Create a passed pawn

Use a majority on one wing to make a pawn that can run to the other side. A passed pawn ties down the opponent and often decides the endgame.

Rook versus minor piece: win it with technique

Being up the exchange is a real edge, worth about 1.5 to 2 pawns. Build calmly, activate your king and use the rook to pick up pawns.

Activate your king in the endgame

In the endgame the king is a strong piece, not a target. Bring it to the centre to support your pawns and block the opponent.

Push your passed pawn (PPP)

If you have a pawn that can promote, support it and push it towards the other side to decide the game.

Put your pawns on the opposite colour of the enemy bishop

If the opponent has a bishop in the endgame, put your pawns and pieces on the other colour (dark squares if his bishop runs on light). The bishop then has nothing to attack.

Mate with king and queen

Shrink the box around the enemy king with your queen a knight's move away, bring your own king up, and finish with the 'kiss of death' or a corner mate. Check for stalemate on every move.

Trade queens when you are a pawn up

With a pawn extra, trading queens is often a good way to simplify and make a setback unlikely.

Pieces first, then pawns

In the endgame, activate your pieces and king before you push your pawns.

Fewer pawn islands

Fewer pawn islands are easier to defend. Keep your pawns connected.

Beware opposite-coloured bishops

Be careful trading into an endgame with opposite-coloured bishops when you are only one pawn up. The defender can often draw easily.

Castling is not automatic without queens

When queens are off, your king is in less danger. Do not castle out of habit: use the king in the centre to fight and support pawns.

Doubled isolated pawns are a target

Aim for positions where the opponent gets doubled, isolated pawns. They are hard to defend and easy to attack in the endgame.

Shelter your king from checks

In rook endings, put your king behind your own pawns so the enemy rook cannot keep giving checks.

Watch out for stalemate

When your pawn is about to promote and the enemy king is boxed in, make sure your opponent still has a legal move.

Stay careful until the last move

Even in a won endgame one slip can throw away the win or allow a draw. Keep calculating and look for the cleanest forced finish.

March a pawn majority

With a pawn majority on the queenside (4 against 2, for example), use it to advance your pawns step by step into a passed pawn and force your opponent into passive defence.

Trade off a passive defender

If the enemy rook stands passively on the back rank to defend, force exchanges that centralize your own pieces and steer towards the endgame.
